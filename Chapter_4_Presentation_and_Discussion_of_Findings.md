# CHAPTER 4: PRESENTATION AND DISCUSSION OF FINDINGS

## 4.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the findings of the study based on data collected through semi-structured interviews with teachers, headmasters, and parents, as well as focus group discussions with learners. The findings are organized according to the study objectives and are discussed in relation to the literature review in Chapter 2.

The study used a sample of:
- 6 teachers (T1-T6)
- 3 headmasters (H1-H3)
- 30 learners grouped into 3 focus groups (FG1, FG2, FG3)
- 9 parents (P1-P9)

The chapter therefore reports the findings on the disciplinary procedures used, learners' perceptions, effectiveness, challenges, and strategies for improvement.

---

## 4.2 Presentation and Discussion of Findings According to Research Objectives

### Objective 1: To identify the disciplinary procedures used to manage learner behaviour in selected high schools in Bulawayo Central District

#### 4.2.1 Presentation of Findings

The data collected showed that the schools used a combination of procedures to manage learner behaviour. Interviews with teachers and headmasters revealed that the most commonly used procedures included:

1. Verbal warnings
2. Written warnings
3. Detention
4. Temporary removal from class
5. Referral to school authorities
6. Parental involvement
7. Guidance and counselling referrals
8. Suspension for serious offences
9. Restorative measures such as repair of damage or community service

Teachers reported that verbal warnings were generally used as the first response to minor misbehaviour. Detention was commonly used for repeated or more serious offences. Headmasters indicated that serious cases were referred to the school administration, and in serious cases suspension was used as a last resort.

#### 4.2.2 Discussion of Findings

The findings show that schools rely on a gradual system of discipline in which the severity of sanctions increases according to the seriousness and frequency of misconduct. This is consistent with the behavioural principles discussed in Chapter 2, especially Skinner's operant conditioning theory, which argues that behaviour is shaped by the consequences that follow it (Skinner, 1953). This means that when learners are rewarded or corrected appropriately, their behaviour may improve.

The literature review supports the idea that effective disciplinary procedures are fair, predictable, and linked to clear behavioural expectations. UNESCO (2019) notes that discipline is most effective when it is non-violent, consistent and connected to school rules. In the study, schools attempted to apply a structured disciplinary process. However, evidence from the interviews suggests that there were inconsistencies in how the procedures were applied across teachers and classes.

The use of Guidance and Counselling as part of discipline is also supported by the literature. Chingangaidze (2021) notes that counselling can serve as a preventive measure in school discipline, helping learners to understand and correct their behaviour before it escalates. This supports the study's view that discipline should not rely only on punishment; it should also promote reflection, self-control, and behavioural responsibility.

### Objective 2: To examine learners' perceptions of disciplinary procedures used in the school

#### 4.3.1 Presentation of Findings

Learners' views were obtained through focus group discussions. The findings showed that:

- Most learners accepted the need for rules and discipline
- Learners appreciated fairness and respect in discipline
- They were most critical when discipline was inconsistent or humiliating
- Many learners preferred dialogue over harsh punishment
- Some learners felt that the procedures were fair when teachers explained reasons for action

FG1 reported that discipline was fair when teachers listened to learners and explained why certain behaviour was wrong. FG2 indicated that some teachers were harsh and used public humiliation. FG3 suggested that discipline should help learners to learn rather than simply punish them.

In general, 18 out of 30 learners felt that school disciplinary procedures were fair. However, 12 out of 30 learners said the procedures were either inconsistent or too harsh. Many learners emphasised that discipline should be respectful, educational, and transparent.

#### 4.3.2 Discussion of Findings

The findings are consistent with the literature reviewed in Chapter 2. Osher et al. (2018) argue that learners are more likely to cooperate when discipline is respectful, clear, and fair. The study found that learners respected teachers who explained disciplinary action and listened to their side of the story. This suggests that discipline becomes more effective when learners perceive it as just and educative rather than punitive.

The responses also confirm that learners distinguish between corrective discipline and harsh punishment. This is consistent with the argument in the literature that disciplinary procedures can motivate learners when they are consistent and focused on guiding behaviour rather than humiliating learners. As Malchiodi (2020) notes, learner behaviour is shaped by emotions, relationships, and personal experiences; therefore, punitive methods alone may not lead to lasting behaviour change.

### Objective 3: To assess the effectiveness of school disciplinary procedures in enhancing positive learner behaviour

#### 4.4.1 Presentation of Findings

Teachers and headmasters indicated that disciplinary procedures were effective to some extent in maintaining school order and reducing disruptive behaviour. Headmasters reported that verbal warnings and detention were useful for minor cases, while suspension was used for serious offences. However, most participants noted that long-term behaviour change was limited when disciplinary procedures were not accompanied by counselling and parental involvement.

The findings from focus groups showed that many learners improved their behaviour when rules were clear and consequences were explained. Nevertheless, learners also noted that strict punishment alone often resulted in resentment and repeat misconduct.

The average pattern from participant responses showed that:
- short-term compliance was achieved in many cases
- repeat offences still occurred among some learners
- discipline was more effective when combined with counselling and parental support

#### 4.4.2 Discussion of Findings

This finding supports the literature review that school discipline is most effective when it is educational and consistent. Korpershoek et al. (2016) found that school-wide behaviour programmes and classroom management strategies can improve learner engagement and reduce misconduct. Similarly, McCluskey et al. (2019) observe that restorative and relationship-based approaches can reduce exclusion and improve behaviour when properly implemented.

The data also supports the idea that disciplinary procedures are most effective when learners understand the reasons for the rules and consequences. In this study, learners who viewed discipline as fair and explanatory were more likely to accept it and improve behaviour. This is aligned with the Behaviourism theory and the broader view that consequences must be predictable and logically linked to conduct.

However, the findings suggest that punitive measures alone are insufficient. As noted in the literature, punishment may produce short-term compliance but not lasting behavioural change. Therefore, the research indicates that discipline should be reinforced with mentoring, counselling, restorative dialogue, and parental cooperation.

### Objective 4: To examine the challenges associated with implementing school disciplinary procedures

#### 4.5.1 Presentation of Findings

Teachers identified several challenges affecting the implementation of disciplinary procedures. These included:

- inconsistent application of rules by different teachers
- lack of counselling services
- large class sizes
- weak parental cooperation
- unclear policy guidelines
- limited monitoring and follow-up

Headmasters indicated that the main challenges were inconsistency, lack of staff training, limited school resources, and inadequate counselling support. Learners added that some teachers were too harsh, did not listen to them, and did not explain disciplinary decisions.

The recurring theme was that discipline was not always applied uniformly and fairly across the school.

#### 4.5.2 Discussion of Findings

The challenges identified in this study are consistent with the literature reviewed in Chapter 2. UNESCO (2019) and WHO et al. (2019) emphasise that schools in many contexts face resource constraints, inconsistent policy implementation, and limited counselling support. In addition, Noltemeyer et al. (2015) highlight that exclusionary discipline and punitive methods can have negative effects on learners' emotional wellbeing and academic progress.

The findings also align with the view that schools in developing contexts often struggle with over-enrolment and insufficient trained personnel. In Zimbabwe, as noted in the literature, shortages of counsellors and lack of monitoring can weaken the effectiveness of discipline. This study confirms that the absence of a strong Guidance and Counselling structure undermines discipline and reduces trust between learners and teachers.

---

### Objective 5: To proffer strategies for improving school disciplinary procedures necessary to promote positive learner behaviour

#### 4.6.1 Presentation of Findings

Participants suggested several strategies for improving school disciplinary procedures. These included:

1. Training teachers in positive and non-violent behaviour management
2. Strengthening Guidance and Counselling departments
3. Documenting disciplinary procedures and ensuring consistency
4. Involving parents in the process of discipline
5. Using restorative practices and mediation
6. Encouraging learner participation in discussions about rules and conduct
7. Monitoring and evaluating disciplinary cases regularly
8. Using counselling and mentoring as preventive and corrective interventions

Headmasters and teachers emphasized that discipline should be educational, fair, and responsive to individual learner needs.

#### 4.6.2 Discussion of Findings

The strategies highlighted above are strongly supported by the literature review. Soneson et al. (2020) recommend positive behavioural support, social-emotional learning, teacher development, and learner participation as key strategies for improving school behaviour. In Zimbabwe, schools are encouraged to train teachers in non-violent discipline, strengthen counselling, and document disciplinary cases (MoPSE, 2020; ZHRC, 2021).

The findings suggest that effective school discipline should combine consequences with support. A disciplinary system based solely on punishment is likely to produce resistance and repeat misconduct, whereas a system that includes guidance, counselling, parental engagement, and restorative action is more likely to promote positive behaviour.

---

## 4.7 Summary of the Chapter

This chapter presented and discussed the findings of the study according to the five research objectives. The study established that schools in Bulawayo Central District used a range of disciplinary measures, including warnings, detention, suspension, and parental involvement. Learners generally accepted discipline when it was fair, clear, and respectful. However, the study also found that inconsistency, harsh punishment, limited counselling, and weak parental engagement weakened the effectiveness of disciplinary procedures.

The discussion showed that effective discipline should not be based solely on punishment. It should be educational, consistent, and supported by Guidance and Counselling, parental involvement, and restorative practice. These insights will inform the conclusions and recommendations in Chapter 5.
